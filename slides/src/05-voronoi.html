Argmax as a nearest-vertex rule

Argmax sends each $\mathbf{p}$ to its nearest vertex. A Voronoi rule sends it to the nearest of $K$ prototypes under a distance; this work uses the Euclidean one, $\delta_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbf{p}) = \arg\min_{k} \| \mathbf{p} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_k \|_2$ (Definition 6; B29).

  • $\|\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{e}_i\|_2^2 = \|\mathbf{p}\|_2^2 - 2 p_i + 1$: argmax is the nearest-vertex rule. Move the reference points and you get a Voronoi rule with prototypes.
  • At the vertices every distance gives argmax. Euclidean: straight boundaries, which turn with the prototypes. Manhattan: bent boundaries. Hellinger: curved ones.
  • Prototypes in the simplex have $K(K-1)$ parameters.

Learning the prototypes

The prototypes are fitted like the partition matrix, by random search on the validation sample $\mathcal{V}$ (Section 7.4):

  1. start from the argmax prototypes, $\boldsymbol{\mu}_k = \mathbf{e}_k$;
  2. draw a new configuration $\boldsymbol{\mu}_0, \dots, \boldsymbol{\mu}_{K-1}$ (free prototypes can send a pure-class input $\mathbf{e}_k$ to another class);
  3. keep it if it lowers $\hat{R}$ on $\mathcal{V}$, and repeat for a fixed number of draws.
  • Same recipe as for the partition matrix: draw, score on the validation sample, keep the best.
  • The question is which configurations to draw.

The dominant label constraint

A cost matrix is element-wise reasonable if $C_{jj} < C_{ji}$ for every $j$ and all $i \neq j$ (O'Brien et al., 2008). Then the Bayes rule sends each vertex $\mathbf{e}_k$ to class $k$. The dominant label constraint (DLC) requires the same property of the prototypes.

Definition (7, dominant label constraint)

A configuration $\mathcal{M} =(\boldsymbol{\mu}_0, \dots, \boldsymbol{\mu}_{K-1})$ of points of $\Delta^{K-1}$ satisfies the DLC if $\arg\min_{j \in \mathcal{Y}} \|\mathbf{e}_k - \boldsymbol{\mu}_j\|_2 = \{k\}$ for every $k \in \mathcal{Y}$.

  • With an element-wise reasonable matrix, every vertex goes to its own class.
  • The DLC asks the prototypes for the same: on pure-class inputs the Voronoi rule agrees with argmax.
  • Drag $\boldsymbol{\mu}_0$: the shaded area is where the constraint still holds.

Sampling prototypes under the DLC

The random search draws configurations that must satisfy the DLC and still cover the simplex (six samplers: B33, B10). A uniform draw usually violates the DLC. Moving the largest coordinate of the $k$-th draw to position $k$ restores it in almost every draw at $K = 3$, and in fewer as $K$ grows.

  • The same three draws twice: uniform, they send vertices to other classes; after the swap, each prototype lies where its own coordinate is largest.
  • The swap fixes each prototype alone, not the pairs: swap-max keeps the DLC in $61\%$ of the draws at $K = 100$.
  • The sampler adds a test against the earlier prototypes.

Sampling prototypes under the DLC

The sequential $d^2$ sampler keeps the swap and adds a test against the earlier prototypes. With $d^2$ the squared distance, for $i = 0, \dots, K - 1$:

  • draw $\mathbf{p}$ uniformly on the simplex, its largest coordinate swapped into position $i$;
  • accept it as $\boldsymbol{\mu}_i$ if $d^2(\mathbf{e}_k, \mathbf{p}) > d^2(\mathbf{e}_k, \boldsymbol{\mu}_k)$ and $d^2(\mathbf{e}_i, \mathbf{p}) < d^2(\mathbf{e}_i, \boldsymbol{\mu}_k)$ for every $k < i$; else draw again.

Every configuration it returns satisfies the DLC (Proposition 7.5.1; P8).

  • Dashed disc: the new prototype must stay out of it, or it would take an earlier vertex. Dotted disc: it must enter it, so that its own vertex is nearest to it.
  • Shaded: where a draw is kept; crosses: rejected draws, rare at $K = 3$.
  • Of the six samplers compared, it is the only one that keeps the DLC for every $K$ up to $100$ and still explores the interior (B10).

Expressivity of the Voronoi class

  • $\mathcal{H}_{Vor}$, the Voronoi rules with prototypes anywhere in $\mathbb{R}^K$, strictly contains the parallel-shift class: $\mathcal{H}_{PS} \subsetneq \mathcal{H}_{Vor}$ (Proposition 7.3.2; B31).
  • $\mathcal{H}_{Vor}^{\mathrm{DLC}}$, with prototypes in the simplex under the DLC, is the class of the experiments. Its containment is not proved, so the theorem compares the best risks.
Theorem (7.3.1, strict risk separation from the parallel-shift class; proof: P7)

There exist a data distribution $\mathbb{P}$ and a cost matrix $\mathbf{C}$ such that $\inf_{\delta \in \mathcal{H}_{Vor}^{\mathrm{DLC}}} R(\delta) < \inf_{\delta \in \mathcal{H}_{PS}} R(\delta)$.

  • Unconstrained prototypes contain every parallel shift; the experiments use constrained ones.
  • For the constrained class: a distribution where it has strictly lower risk than every shift.

Expressivity of the Voronoi class

Take $K = 3$, $0/1$ loss, and mass $1/3$ on each of $\mathbf{p}_C = (0.8, 0.1, 0.1)$, $\mathbf{p}_A = (0.4, 0.3, 0.3)$ and $\mathbf{p}_B = (0, 0.5, 0.5)$, with labels $0$, $1$, $2$.

A parallel-shift rule picks the smallest score $s_k(\mathbf{p}) = \mathbf{c}_k^\top \mathbf{p} + \alpha_k$. On the altitude $p_1 = p_2$, $s_1 - s_2$ is the constant $\alpha_1 - \alpha_2$, so no such rule labels $\mathbf{p}_A$ as $1$ and $\mathbf{p}_B$ as $2$, and its risk is at least $1/3$.

The prototypes $\boldsymbol{\mu}_0 = \mathbf{e}_0$, $\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 = (0.4, 0.5, 0.1)$, $\boldsymbol{\mu}_2 = (0.1, 0.4, 0.5)$ satisfy the DLC and classify all three points, with risk $0$.

  • Three points on one altitude of the simplex.
  • No parallel shift labels $\mathbf{p}_A$ as $1$ and $\mathbf{p}_B$ as $2$.
  • The Voronoi rule classifies all three: risk $0$ against at least $1/3$.

A scenario built for rotation

  • The scenario of Section 7.6 is built to need rotation. Classes $0$ and $1$ have correlations $+0.8$ and $-0.8$ between the first two features, and class $2$ has $0.8$ between the first and the third. Naive Bayes ignores correlations, so its error turns the boundaries in different directions.
  • A shift (c) translates the boundaries without turning them, and a scaling (b) turns them through coupled parameters. The prototypes (d) and the full matrix (e) can turn each boundary separately.
  • Both improve accuracy over argmax (a) by about $2$ points (B19).
  • What makes the case special: it is constructed so that each boundary needs its own rotation.
  • That is when prototypes help; under $0/1$-type objectives, and as an existence example.
  • In the benchmark the Voronoi rule does not lead.

Capacity of the hypothesis classes

For $K$ classes the bound uses the Natarajan dimension $d_{Nat}$ (Daniely, Sabato, et al., 2015; Natarajan, 1989): $R(\delta) \leq \hat{R}(\delta) + c \, C_{max} \sqrt{d_{Nat}(\mathcal{H}_{dec}) / m}$ (Remark 7.7.1; definition on B8). With prototypes anywhere, the Voronoi rules are exactly the full-matrix rules (Proposition 7.2.1; P6). $\mathcal{H}_{Vor}^{\Delta}$ denotes those with prototypes in the simplex.

Theorem (7.7.2 to 7.7.5; proofs: P9, P10)

\begin{align*} d_{Nat}(\mathcal{H}_{PS}) &= K - 1 , & d_{Nat}(\mathcal{H}_{Sc}) &\leq K - 1 , \\ d_{Nat}(\mathcal{H}_{Vor}) = d_{Nat}(\mathcal{H}_{Full}) &\leq K^2 , & d_{Nat}(\mathcal{H}_{Vor}^{\Delta}) &\leq K^2 . \end{align*}

The parallel-shift value is exact for an admissible $\mathbf{C}$, one where every class is the unique risk minimizer at some point of the simplex (B30); the other values are upper bounds (against $K$: B18).

  • Parallel shift: exactly $K - 1$.
  • The others are upper bounds of order $K^2$.
  • No capacity argument favours Voronoi over the full matrix: with free prototypes they are the same class.
